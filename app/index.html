How Hoshimachi works

星待ち, waiting for the stars

Darkness maps tell you where the sky is dark, and forecasts cover the next few days. Neither tells you, weeks ahead, how likely a trip is to get you a usable night. Hoshimachi estimates that base rate by replaying ten years of real weather through the exact darkness and moon hours of your dates.

What counts as a good night

  1. Dark site. The region's darkest ~1 km spot meets your Bortle limit. Sky brightness is modelled from VIIRS satellite night lights spread over 150 km with Walker's law (glow ∝ distance−2.5), converted to zenith mag/arcsec² and then to a Bortle class.
  2. Dark, moon-free hours. Sun at least 18° below the horizon, and the moon either below the horizon or under 25% lit. In Milky Way mode the galactic core must also be 10° up. These are calculated exactly for your dates and the region's darkest spot.
  3. Clear sky. At least the chosen number of hours with total cloud under 25% inside those hours, in a row or with gaps if you allow passing clouds.

How the odds are estimated

  • A base rate, not a forecast. A clear night on your dates in a past year says nothing about this year. What carries over is the season: rainy season, the winter monsoon on the Sea of Japan side, clear Pacific-side winters. So the tool pools every past year and a ±30-day window around your dates (up to 610 past trips) and counts how often at least one night worked. Replaying real sequences keeps the fact that bad weather comes in multi-day systems.
  • Shrinkage. Each region is blended 75/25 with the average of its neighbouring ~28 km cells. Small samples exaggerate extremes. Blending pulls them back and made the odds better calibrated in testing.
  • Uncertainty. The range shown is a 95% interval from how much the odds vary between years (about ±10 points typically). Neighbouring days in one year share weather, so the past trips are not independent samples. Regions whose ranges overlap the top result are effectively tied.
  • No peeking. If your dates fall inside 2015–2024, that year is left out of the odds and shown separately as what actually happened.

Reading the colours

Warm colours always mean artificial light: on the darkness and light-trend layers and the light-dome compass, brighter and warmer means more light pollution. Cool blue-to-mint always means clear-sky odds, brighter meaning better. Every scale gets lighter as its value rises, so it still reads in greyscale and for colour-blind viewers (Crameri et al., Nature Communications, 2020). The interface stays dim and warm because night vision is most sensitive to blue-green light. Red light mode goes further for use in the field.

Limitations

  • Not a forecast. The odds know nothing about this particular season (El Niño, a late rainy season). Within a week of your trip, use a live forecast.
  • 10 years is short. Rare patterns may be missing, and a changing climate could shift cloudiness later.
  • Clouds. ERA5 reanalysis gives one value per ~28 km cell per hour. Valley fog, sea fog, mountain-top cloud and fast-moving cumulus are smoothed out. Thin high cloud counts as cloud. The 25% threshold is fixed. "Passing clouds OK" mode was not separately validated.
  • Darkness. Calibrated to one published ground reading (Yonago, 18.8 mag/arcsec²). Terrain shielding, altitude and natural airglow are ignored, and Bortle is really a visual scale, so labels may be off by one class. The satellite under-sees white LEDs, so darkness may be overstated near LED-lit towns and the light-trend layer understates brightening.
  • Light domes. Direction and strength come from the same distance model, without hills that may hide a town.
  • Moon. The 25%-lit cut-off is a simplification. A low crescent and a high quarter moon are treated alike.
  • Months view. Uses mid-month darkness hours for the whole month and ignores the moon.
  • Places. The darkest spot is a satellite pixel, not a surveyed or accessible site. Distances are straight lines; islands need ferries or flights. English place names are auto-romanised (Japanese names are authoritative) and spot markers are approximate.
